First-Order Radioactive Decay: Remaining Fraction and Amount

Audience: Second-year engineering undergraduate who knows exponentials, logarithms, and simple rates of change.

Plan: Teach the first-order decay law N(t)=N0 exp(-lambda t) by letting learners set N0, lambda, and t, then showing the remaining fraction, remaining amount, half-life, and elapsed half-lives. The page compares deterministic bulk behavior to the half-life time scale.

Starting point

Idea: For a fixed decay constant, the remaining fraction of a radioactive sample falls exponentially in time, and the half-life sets the time scale.

Why it matters: It explains why bulk radioactive sources lose power smoothly and predictably, as in Cooper's RTG model.

Explanation: From initial amount N0 (a.u.), decay constant lambda (1/y), and elapsed time t (y): first compute half-life t_half = ln(2)/lambda, or a large finite sentinel when lambda = 0. Next compute remaining fraction f = exp(-lambda t). Then compute remaining amount N(t) = N0 * f. Finally compute t/t_half to compare elapsed time to half-life. Because f depends only on lambda and t, changing N0 scales N(t) without changing f or t_half.

Guided explorations

Halving the initial amount

Predict: Predict: will the remaining fraction change if N0 is halved? Then apply the preset, which sets n0, lambda, and t.

Observe: The remaining fraction f stays at 0.6065 and half-life t_half stays at 6.931 y, while the remaining amount N(t) drops from 60.65 a.u. to 30.33 a.u.; ratio t/t_half stays at 0.721.

Explain: Because f = exp(-lambda t) does not contain N0, halving N0 halves N(t) = N0 * f but leaves f, t_half, and ratio unchanged.

Doubling the decay constant

Predict: Predict: how does doubling lambda change the remaining fraction f, the half-life t_half, and the elapsed half-lives t/t_half? Then apply the preset, which sets lambda, n0, and t.

Observe: The remaining fraction f falls from 0.6065 to 0.3679, half-life t_half falls from 6.931 y to 3.466 y, remaining amount N(t) falls to 36.79 a.u., and ratio t/t_half rises from 0.721 to 1.443.

Explain: Both f = exp(-lambda t) and t_half = ln(2)/lambda depend inversely on lambda, so a larger lambda makes the decay faster and the half-life shorter.

Grounding

  • Cooper (2008), Searching for modifications to the exponential radioactive decay law with the Cassini spacecraft — PDF p. 1, paragraph beginning The thermal power output of an RTG excerpt

    The paper writes P_th(t) = N0 lambda exp(-lambda t) E_d, so the exponential factor exp(-lambda t) is the decay law used for the radioisotope source.

  • Cooper (2008), Searching for modifications to the exponential radioactive decay law with the Cassini spacecraft — brief focus and excerpt simplification

    The page drops E_d and the lambda factor from Cooper's thermal-power expression to isolate the remaining amount N(t) = N0 exp(-lambda t), and treats half-life as a teaching derivation rather than a measured result.

  • Cooper (2008), Searching for modifications to the exponential radioactive decay law with the Cassini spacecraft — abstract excerpt

    The paper reports no significant deviations from exponential decay over a range of 0.7 to 1.6 A.U.

Limitation

The deterministic curve describes expected bulk behavior of a large population; it does not predict whether one specific nucleus decays in a given interval. For lambda = 0 the code uses a large finite sentinel (1e6 y) for half-life so outputs stay finite, but the true half-life is infinite.
