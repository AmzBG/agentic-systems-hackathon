Beta-Binomial Updating of an Unknown Success Probability

Audience: Second-year engineering undergraduate comfortable with fractions, averages, and elementary probability.

Plan: Learners set a Beta prior (prior alpha, prior beta) and a count of observed successes and failures, then watch the conjugate Beta-Binomial update produce the posterior parameters, the data weight, and the posterior mean, comparing prior and posterior visually.

Starting point

Idea: A Beta prior with parameters prior alpha and prior beta is updated by successes and failures simply by adding: posterior alpha equals prior alpha plus successes and posterior beta equals prior beta plus failures, so the posterior mean is a weighted blend of the prior mean and the observed proportion.

Why it matters: It gives a concrete, closed-form picture of how prior belief and new evidence combine when the quantity of interest is an unknown probability, which is the inference question Bayes posed for an unknown event.

Explanation: Start from prior alpha and prior beta. The prior mean is prior alpha divided by prior alpha plus prior beta. The observed successes and failures are then added to the prior pseudo-counts: posterior alpha equals prior alpha plus successes, and posterior beta equals prior beta plus failures. The data weight is the total observed count (successes plus failures) divided by the total count (prior alpha plus prior beta plus successes plus failures); it measures how much of the posterior comes from data rather than prior. The posterior mean is posterior alpha divided by posterior alpha plus posterior beta, which equals posterior alpha divided by that same total count. Finally the shift is the posterior mean minus the prior mean, showing the direction and size of the update.

Guided explorations

Many observations: the data take over

Predict: Predict: with 18 successes and 6 failures, will the posterior mean readout sit near the observed proportion 0.75 or near the prior mean 0.5? Then apply the preset, which sets prior alpha to 2, prior beta to 2, observed successes s to 18 and observed failures f to 6.

Observe: The posterior alpha readout becomes 20 and the posterior beta readout becomes 8, so the prior and posterior parameter bars are far apart. The data weight readout is 0.8571, and the posterior mean readout moves from 0.5 to 0.7143, giving a shift of 0.2143; the swept line also flattens near 0.71 as successes rise.

Explain: The two observations of prior alpha and prior beta are only 4 pseudo-counts, while 24 observations are added to the posterior parameters, so the data weight (s plus f) divided by the total count is 24/28. Because the posterior mean is posterior alpha divided by the total, it lands close to the observed proportion rather than at the prior mean.

One observation, strong prior: the prior barely moves

Predict: Predict: with a prior mean of 0.8 and a single success, how far will the posterior mean readout move? Then apply the preset, which sets prior alpha to 8, prior beta to 2, observed successes s to 1 and observed failures f to 0.

Observe: The posterior alpha readout becomes 9 while the posterior beta readout stays at 2, so the parameter bars for prior and posterior almost coincide. The data weight readout is only 0.0769, the posterior mean readout creeps from 0.8 to 0.8182, and the shift readout is just 0.0182.

Explain: The prior contributes 10 pseudo-counts and the single observation contributes 1, so the data weight is 1/13 and the posterior mean, posterior alpha divided by the total count 11, is pulled only slightly above the prior mean. The conjugate update can only move the posterior mean by as much as the data share allows.

Grounding

  • An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances (Bayes, 1763) — PROBLEM statement, p. 3 excerpt

    Bayes poses the problem of finding the chance that the probability of an unknown event lies between two named degrees of probability, given the number of times it has happened and failed.

  • An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances (Bayes, 1763) — Section II, Proposition 10 and Rule 1 (as cited in the brief) unverified

    The essay derives a rule for inference about an unknown event probability from observed successes and failures, but it does not use the modern alpha and beta parameterization or the Beta-Binomial identity shown here.

  • An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances (Bayes, 1763) — this page's construction simplification

    The conjugate update posterior alpha equals prior alpha plus s and posterior beta equals prior beta plus f, together with the illustrative slider values and counts used here, is a modern teaching simplification of the inference problem and is not presented as the essay's own computation.

Limitation

This page assumes independent trials with one fixed unknown success probability and a Beta prior, so it is a conjugate-model teaching device, not a reproduction of any numerical result in Bayes's essay, which used a different parameterization and a uniform prior over the unknown probability. The total count prior alpha plus prior beta plus successes plus failures is always at least 1, so every division is defined; when there are no observations the data weight is 0 and the posterior mean equals the prior mean exactly, and the sweep fixes failures and the prior at their current values.
